Agenda 21, the United Nations' Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), and its associated agenda-setting process are complex concepts that have been subject to various interpretations. Here's a breakdown of what Agenda 21 is all about:

**What is Agenda 21?**

Agenda 21 was adopted by the United Nations in September 1992, as part of the United Nations Conference on Environment and Development (UNCED). The goal of Agenda 21 is to address global environmental issues through sustainable development. It's a comprehensive framework that aims to achieve three interrelated goals:

1. **Environmental protection**: Reducing greenhouse gas emissions, conserving biodiversity, and protecting natural resources.
2. **Economic growth**: Promoting sustainable economic development, reducing poverty, and increasing access to basic services like healthcare and education.

**What are the SDGs?**

Agenda 21's agenda is built around the United Nations' Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) framework. The SDGs are a set of 17 goals that aim to address pressing global issues such as:

1. **Poverty reduction**
2. **Healthcare and human rights**
3. **Education, science, technology, and innovation**
4. **Economic growth, productivity, and employment**
5. **Environmental protection** (including climate change)
6. **Social protection** (including poverty reduction)

The SDGs are divided into four pillars:

1. **People's rights**: Protecting human dignity and well-being.
2. **Economy**: Promoting sustainable economic growth and development.
3. **Environment**: Protecting the natural environment for future generations.
4. **Industry, innovation, and infrastructure**: Fostering innovation, investment, and infrastructure.

**What is Agenda 21's role?**

Agenda 21 serves as a guiding framework for decision-making at all levels of government (national, regional, local) to achieve the SDGs. It provides a set of principles, indicators, and targets that help policymakers and stakeholders understand how to address global challenges.

In practice, Agenda 21 has been used as a tool for:

1. **Setting goals**: Establishing specific objectives and targets for achieving the SDGs.
2. **Identifying priorities**: Prioritizing actions based on their potential impact on human well-being and environmental sustainability.
3. **Monitoring progress**: Tracking progress toward achieving the SDG goals.

**Criticisms and limitations**

While Agenda 21 has contributed significantly to global discussions around sustainable development, it is not without its criticisms:

1. **Lack of enforcement mechanisms**: The framework relies heavily on voluntary commitments from governments and stakeholders.
2. **Inequitable distribution of resources**: Some argue that the SDGs do not address issues like climate change disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations or developing countries.
3. **Limited scope for action**: Critics argue that Agenda 21 focuses primarily on environmental protection, neglecting social and economic aspects.
