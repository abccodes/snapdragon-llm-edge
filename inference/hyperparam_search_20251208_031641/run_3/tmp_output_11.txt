The reason for this discrepancy lies in how light behaves when it enters Earth's atmosphere. When sunlight passes through our atmosphere, it encounters tiny molecules of gases like nitrogen and oxygen. These molecules scatter the shorter wavelengths of light, such as blue and violet, more than the longer wavelengths, like red and orange.

This phenomenon is known as Rayleigh scattering. It's named after Lord Rayleigh, who first described the effect in the late 19th century. The amount of scattering that occurs depends on the wavelength of light: shorter wavelengths are scattered more than longer wavelengths.

As a result, when sunlight enters Earth's atmosphere, it appears blue to our eyes because the shorter wavelengths (like blue and violet) are scattered away by the gas molecules, while the longer wavelengths (red and orange) continue to travel in a straight line. This is why we see the sun as more of a yellowish or golden color during sunrise and sunset.
