**Categorical vs. Existential**

The phrase "Is every cat a cat?" is often seen as an example of a categorical statement. A categorical statement is one that asserts something about all members of a category or group. In this case, the statement says that every cat is indeed a cat.

However, existential statements are different. They assert existence rather than identity. For instance: "There exists a cat." This statement does not claim that cats exist in general; it simply states that there is at least one cat.

**Modal**

The phrase "Is every cat...?" also contains modal verbs like "is" and "there is". These are called modal verbs because they express possibility or necessity. In this context, the statement says something about a possible future situation: "There may be cats."

In contrast to existential statements, which assert existence, modal statements express possibilities or necessities.

**Linguistic Implications**

The use of categorical and existential/modal language in our everyday conversation can have implications for how we think about reality. For example:

* If I say that every cat is a cat, I'm asserting the existence of cats as a category.
* However, if I say there may be cats, I'm expressing possibility rather than necessity.

**Philosophical Implications**

The debate surrounding "Is every cat a cat?" has implications for various philosophical theories:

* **Ontological realism**: If we accept that every cat is indeed one kind of thing (a category), this supports ontological realism. This theory posits that reality consists of categories or types, rather than individual objects.
* **Nominalism**: On the other hand, if we consider "every cat" as a mere label without any inherent existence, this challenges nominalism. Nominalists argue that words like "cat" refer to nothing more than labels.
