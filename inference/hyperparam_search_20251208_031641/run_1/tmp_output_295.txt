According to Baumeister's ego-depletion experiments, individuals experience a decrease in their ability to perform tasks when they are exhausted or depleted (i.e., "ego depletion"). This concept is often referred to as the "depletion hypothesis." The depletions refer to the reduction of an individual's capacity for self-control and motivation. As Baumeister argues, this depletion can lead individuals to engage in less desirable behaviors, such as procrastination, distraction, or even aggression.
The depletions are thought to be caused by a combination of factors, including:
1. Reduced motivation: When an individual is depleted, they may experience decreased motivation and interest in the task at hand.
2. Decreased self-control: The depletion can lead to a decrease in self-control, making it more difficult for individuals to regulate their behavior.
3. Increased impulsivity: Depletion can also increase impulsive behavior, as individuals may be less able to delay gratification or consider alternative solutions.

The depletions have been found to occur across various domains, including work, social relationships, and personal goals. Baumeister's theory suggests that the depletion hypothesis is a fundamental aspect of human motivation and behavior regulation.
