 This question is often used to illustrate the concept of identity and how it relates to personal characteristics, such as behavior or appearance. However, this question also raises questions about what makes us who we are.

What does "being a person" mean? Is it just a set of behaviors, habits, and traits that define our existence? Or is there something more fundamental?

One way to approach this question is by considering the concept of selfhood. Selfhood refers to the idea that an individual has a unique identity or essence that defines who they are. This can be understood in various ways, such as:

1. **Biological definition**: From a biological perspective, our identities are shaped by our genes, hormones, and brain chemistry. Our physical appearance, behavior, and cognitive abilities all contribute to what makes us "us."
2. **Psychological definition**: In the realm of psychology, selfhood is often associated with personality traits, values, and beliefs that define an individual's character.
3. **Social constructionism**: This perspective suggests that our identities are constructed through social interactions, relationships, and cultural norms.

The question of what makes us "who we are" raises questions about how identity emerges and evolves over time. Is it:

1. **Innate or acquired?** Do our traits and characteristics develop naturally, or do they emerge through experiences and learning?
2. **Fixed or fluid?** Are our identities fixed at birth, or can they change as we grow and adapt to new situations?

Philosophers have debated these questions for centuries, with various theories attempting to explain the nature of selfhood:

1. **Essentialism**: This perspective posits that an individual's identity is determined by their essential characteristics, such as their genetic makeup.
2. **Nominalism**: In contrast, nominalists argue that our identities are mere labels or categories we assign to ourselves and others based on social norms and conventions.

Ultimately, the question of what makes us "who we are" remains a complex and multifaceted one. While biology, psychology, and social constructionism offer different perspectives, they all acknowledge the intricate relationships between identity, behavior, appearance, and culture.
